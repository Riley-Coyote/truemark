In short

Keep Motion 4’s story and replace its set with our photographs. The camera starts in the pedestal room you approved, with five vials on desktop and three on phone. Your thumb moves it in on one vial: BPC-157, moved to the centre pedestal.

Motion 4: what to keep, what to leave

Codex’s study, recorded chapter by chapter at desktop and phone sizes.

Keep

  • The story order: the vial, its label, its lot number, its certificate. It tells the brand’s own idea, lot-level traceability, in four steps.
  • One idea per screen, at a calm pace.
  • Soft, bright, even light.
  • The label treated as something worth reading.

Leave

  • The set. Its plinths and backdrop are drawn by computer, and beside our photographed room they read as a render.
  • Flat vials that always face the camera and float when they lift.
  • A soft label at the moment it asks you to read it.
  • White app cards after the label, and coloured markers that mean nothing.
  • Text colliding on two chapters.
  • Guided scrolling. One swipe plays a fixed move, so you can’t scrub, and it fights an iPhone’s momentum.
  • On phone, the outer vials are cut off, cards cover the vial, and the certificate shrinks to a thumbnail.
  • A finale that leads nowhere.

How it moves

  • Your thumb drives it. Scrolling stays normal from top to bottom. Nothing snaps and nothing takes over, so an iPhone’s momentum works as usual.
  • Pauses are built in. At each shot the picture holds still while the page keeps moving under your thumb, which gives time to read.
  • Always a way out. The header, View catalog, a Skip to the collection link and a thin progress line stay in view. Today a phone reaches the cards after about three screens; with the sequence it takes about five.
  • A Motion switch. A visible on and off control. It also closes a gap on the page today: the hero’s mist keeps moving with no way to pause it, and WCAG 2.2.2 asks for one.
  • Reduced motion. People who turn motion off get the same story as still frames, with the same words.
  • Screen readers hear the story once, as text, with a heading for each shot.
  • Taps work when the page is still. A tap during momentum only stops the scroll. So the lot number opens its own page, and Back returns to the same frame.
  • Good at 30 frames a second. iPhones in Low Power Mode hold web pages to 30, so every move has to read well there too.
  • Light to load. It builds on the existing camera move, uses blur prepared ahead of time, and leaves out Motion 4’s 3D engine, which adds 134 KB for about ten shapes.

What I cut, and why

  • The shine sliding across the glass. You called it “really cheap” on Oct 7.
  • The label peeling off the vial. It looks like relabelling, which is exactly what lot tracing exists to rule out.
  • The switch to the single-pedestal photos in the middle of the scroll. They show a different room: one window, pink light on the right, a higher horizon. A one-second tap hides that, but a scroll can stop on any frame. The tap-a-vial move keeps the switch, as you asked on Oct 7.
  • Parallax layers cut from the photo. Their edges stretch and give the photo away.
  • The lot timeline and a separate record screen inside the sequence. They stop the camera. The timeline moves below the cards, and the certificate already carries the record.
  • Typing inside the scene. An iPhone keyboard would jolt the camera, so the lot lookup sits below.
  • Guided scrolling. It fights an iPhone’s momentum.

Why BPC-157

  • It is already the site’s example lot, in the paper trail, the lookup and the certificate card: lot TM-BPC10-2609-01, certificate TB-20260924-VKKT-01, 99.51% by HPLC.
  • Its label can be redrawn sharp from the client’s label file in the brand kit, build_175.py, which I read and never run. The first test checks that it lines up with the photo.
  • From the centre pedestal, the push goes straight in on every screen.
  • Not Retatrutide, Motion 4’s choice. The FDA named it in warning letters sent on 24 August 2026 to five peptide sellers, and the letters judged each site by its overall impression; a research-use-only label didn’t protect them (a law firm’s summary). It shouldn’t be the face of the page’s most-watched moment.
  • Not NAD+, today’s centre vial. The lab’s photo of its tested sample shows a brown vial with a navy cap, far from our clear vial with a white cap.

Questions for the client

None of these stop the build. They need answers before the certificate shot goes live.

  1. Which cap colour ships? The lab’s photo of the tested BPC-157 sample shows a blue cap. Our vial pictures show white caps.
  2. The testing claims. The page says the certificate covers endotoxin and sterility, and lists HPLC · MS. KMD’s certificate says sterility and endotoxin were not assessed, and identity was checked by retention time and UV. On Oct 7 you ruled this is the client’s to settle. The new sequence puts the two side by side, so it’s worth asking now.
  3. The appearance row. The certificate’s specification reads “White lyophilized powder”, the result reads “Clear, colorless”, and the row is marked Conforms.
  4. Tesamorelin in the hero. The same FDA letters named it.
  5. Showing the certificate. It says it may not be reproduced except in full. Is a camera move across it, ending on the whole page, acceptable?
  6. The original label files. The brand kit’s 04_Vial_Labels folder is missing. Nice to have.

Proving it first

  1. The label test. About an hour, with no browser. Redraw BPC-157’s label from the client’s file and lay it over the vial photo, flat and wrapped. It settles the featured vial, the sharp label and the turn.
  2. The first slice, on the pedestal-hero build: the room, the push, the label and the lot number. Both hand-off versions sit behind a switch, and a version with real depth sits beside the plain zoom.
  3. A real iPhone. The Mac’s simulator can’t judge smoothness. The bar: no more than one frame in twenty late, no stall over 50 milliseconds when shots change, and no reloads in twenty runs. Getting the build onto your phone needs your OK to share the dev server on your Wi-Fi, or a private build.
  4. Then the rest: the certificate, the pull-back, the cards and the page below them.

Key tensions

One continuous camera, or an honest cut

Interactive Director: hold the lot number still while the background changes inside focus pulls. When a thumb controls the timing, a cut fired at a set scroll point looks like a glitch.

Art Director and Engineer: one hard cut pretends nothing, reads the same at 30 frames a second, and no one can stop halfway through it. The certificate sets the number in another typeface over two lines, so the match holds position and height while the letter shapes differ.

In cross-examination the Director and the Art Director swapped sides, which suggests both versions have real merit.

Resolution: a question of feel. Build both behind a switch in the first slice and let Riley choose on his phone. Lean: the held number as live, tappable text, with the background change hidden at the bottom of a full defocus and a small dead zone so it can’t flicker.

Real depth, or an honest zoom

Engineer and Director: project the photo onto the room’s measured shapes so the floor and mist gain real depth. A slow, scrubbed zoom on a still can look like pinch-zooming a photo.

Art Director: moving the camera uncovers about 100 pixels of wall the photo never saw, and the generated room has no single exact camera. Mostly zoom, with a little camera movement.

In cross-examination the Engineer conceded the gaps, about 13 points at twice the size, and named the zoom as the fallback.

Resolution: zoom by default. Try the projection in the slice, compare the two unlabelled on an iPhone, and step frame by frame along the pedestal edges.

The turn

Director and Engineer: a small spin, 20 degrees or less, of the rebuilt label under the photo’s own light. On a real spinning vial the shading stays still and only the print slides.

Art Director: the photographed labels are drawn flat across the front. A real label this size would wrap about 210 degrees, so any turn either jumps or slides print across glass, which reads as CG.

Resolution: the overlay test settles it by wrapping the rebuilt label on a cylinder over the photo. If it fails, the focus moves across the label instead.

Blind spots

  • Whose words. Motion 4’s chapter lines were written by Codex, not the client. The sequence needs the client’s copy or lines they approve.
  • The page’s overall impression. The FDA letters judged whole sites. Beyond which vial to feature, nobody weighed how a cinematic treatment of one compound adds to that impression.
  • Tapping a vial during the story. The tap-a-vial move and the scroll story drive the same camera. Nobody said what a tap does mid-push.
  • Android. Every performance claim was made for iPhone.

Round one

What I checked: flight.ts, Rooms.tsx, the mist code in fog/, Motion 4's preview.tsx and scroll-controller.ts, the asset manifest, the client's label generator, the evidence images, and this project's own iOS notes. Nothing has been timed on a real iPhone, so every budget below is arithmetic.

Budget

  • The label hits a hard limit. The vial cutouts are 578×1112 pixels. On an iPhone screen (3 pixels per point), that is full sharpness at a vial about 371 pt tall, 44% of the screen. Anything bigger is upscaled, which is the blur in label-softness-100pct.jpg.
  • The draft's hand-off is the memory peak. One 4K phone plate takes 33 MB once decoded. At the hand-off the draft holds the sharp hero plate, the single-pedestal plate, full-size blur copies and parallax layers. That is roughly 150–200 MB, often held twice, and it's how a tab silently reloads on a 4 GB iPhone. My build below peaks near 55 MB. It gets there by keeping full resolution only for the one pedestal in focus and storing blur at quarter size, since blurred pixels hold no detail.
  • Three.js can go. It costs 134 KB gzipped, and this scene is about ten shapes.

Frame Path & Scroll Model

  • The existing camera push uses live blur. It animates CSS filter: blur and a masked backdrop-filter. That is fine for a 960 ms click. Tied to every scroll frame, it is where I expect the first dropped frames.
  • Motion 4 takes over touch scrolling. It blocks native touch scrolling (touchmove, passive:false, preventDefault), which kills iPhone momentum. It also smooths progress, so the scene lags the finger by up to about 110 ms. On iPhone the scene should follow the finger 1:1. Smoothing belongs only to notched mouse wheels.
  • The pinned stage needs a fixed height. Size it in lvh and never use innerHeight or dvh for progress. Then Safari's toolbar collapsing mid-scroll moves nothing.
  • No scroll-snap on iPhone. Build the rests into the scroll-to-camera mapping as holds. Let each beat's text scroll in the normal page over the pinned room. It stays smooth even when JavaScript stalls, and VoiceOver, Find and anchor links land in the right places.

iPhone Failure Modes

  • Low Power Mode. It caps page rendering at 30 fps by design (WebKit). "60 fps" only holds outside it, so every move must still read well at 30.
  • The lot lookup inside the pinned room. Focusing a field makes iOS scroll the page and leave it there, per this project's own notes, so the camera would jump. Put inputs after the pin.
  • The phone mist. The hero already runs a live mist simulation on phones at 0.6 pixels per point. Scaled up 4× by the push it turns blocky, and a second WebGL canvas would compete with it for the graphics chip.
  • Switching apps. Coming back can lose the WebGL canvas and leave a black hole. Keep a still image underneath.
  • Stalls on the first scroll. Shader compiles, large texture uploads, and phones that only fetch the sharp plate once a product page opens. Load all of it while the hero sits still.
  • Colour shift. Lilac is where colour-space mismatches show. Compare the page image and the canvas pixel by pixel at the hand-off, on a real phone.

What I'd Build It With

  • One WebGL2 canvas. Project the photo onto the room's measured geometry, which already exists in fog/scenes.ts (floor, wall, pedestal cylinders).
  • Frame 0 is the photograph. At the hero framing that render matches the photo pixel for pixel.
  • Real depth during the push. As the camera moves in, it gets true depth where this shallow room has it: the floor and the mist. The pedestals stand about 4.7–5 camera heights away and the wall only about 5.5. Focus blur by depth comes almost free, and the mist is redrawn from the moving camera.
  • A straight-line camera. It moves straight toward the vial, with no pan, orbit or lift, so the flat vial cutouts never look flat.
  • The label becomes vector. Stop the push at full sharpness. The label then peels off the glass as vector art from the client's own generator (build_175.py, which prints TM-BPC10-2609-01). The unroll is how the label escapes the photo's resolution limit.
  • All text in the page, not the canvas.
  • Cut the dissolve to a different plate (a hidden cut plus a mid-scroll load), the light sweep (reflections barely move in a straight push), and snapping.

Where I expect disagreement. The Director may want guided passages or camera arcs. On iPhone, anything that fights momentum feels broken, and an arc shows the vials are flat. The Art Director may want the single-pedestal plate's light and 4K everywhere. In the close-up the background is out of focus, so resolution only pays where the focus is.
